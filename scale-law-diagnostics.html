Empirical distributions

Connectivity is concentrated, but a scale-free claim is not established

Complementary cumulative distribution functions (CCDFs) are shown on logarithmic axes. The fitted line begins at the data-selected lower cutoff xmin. A straight-looking tail is only a visual cue. Statistical inference uses maximum likelihood, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance, semi-parametric bootstrap goodness-of-fit, and comparison with a truncated lognormal alternative.

Interpretation. A bootstrap p value below 0.10 would reject the fitted power-law tail. Here neither fitted tail is rejected, but only a restricted upper tail is involved and the lognormal alternative is not disfavoured. The defensible conclusion is tail compatibility, not a universal scale-free law.

Calculation and mathematical specification

From L4 embeddings to a tested tail model

The analysis reuses the published L4 semantic graph. No risk card, hierarchy assignment, or graph edge is changed.

  1. Semantic graph.Each L4 card is represented by its English name and definition using the published 1,024-dimensional BGE-M3 embedding. For card i, cosine similarity is sij = (zi · zj)/(‖zi‖2‖zj‖2). The undirected graph is the union of each card's eight nearest neighbours subject to sij ≥ 0.60.
  2. Connectivity measures.Degree is ki = Σj 1[(i,j) ∈ E]. Weighted degree, or strength, is wi = Σj:(i,j)∈E sij. These are semantic-network positions, not risk scores.
  3. Tail selection and estimation.For each candidate lower cutoff xmin with at least 50 observations, the exponent is estimated by maximum likelihood. The continuous estimator is α̂ = 1 + n[Σ ln(xi/xmin)]−1; the discrete degree fit uses the standard continuity-corrected estimator. The selected cutoff minimises the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between empirical and fitted cumulative distributions.
  4. Goodness-of-fit.One thousand deterministic semi-parametric bootstrap samples are generated. Values below xmin are resampled empirically and tail values are drawn from the fitted model. The bootstrap p value is the fraction of synthetic KS distances at least as large as the observed distance.
  5. Alternative model.The same tail observations are fitted to a lower-truncated lognormal model. The log-likelihood ratio is R = Σ[ln pPL(xi) − ln pLN(xi)]; its sign indicates the favoured model and a two-sided Vuong test assesses whether the difference is distinguishable.

Reproducibility and validation

Checks applied before publication

Source integrity

The current website contains 622 unique L4 IDs and 55 L3 categories. SHA-256 fingerprints bind the diagnostics to the exact risk-card JSON, semantic graph, and embedding archive recorded in the downloadable result.

Graph invariants

The independent check reconstructs all 3,807 undirected edges, verifies that Σk = 2|E|, and reproduces each node's degree and weighted degree from the edge list.

Deterministic rerun

The analysis fixes the random seed, bootstrap count, and minimum tail size. Re-running the builder must reproduce the diagnostics JSON byte for byte.

Claim discipline

The page distinguishes a statistically non-rejected tail from proof that the whole network is scale free. This follows the severe-testing approach recommended in the network-science literature.

Meaning and limits

What the result supports, and what it does not

Academic significance

The result treats the taxonomy as a multiplex semantic structure rather than three isolated lists. A small set of highly connected L4 cards can act as conceptual bridges, but the evidence does not justify a strong scale-free-network claim. The finding supports research on cross-domain risk dependence and boundary categories while preserving the human-validated hierarchy.

Practical significance

High-connectivity cards are useful prompts for service-level risk discovery because they point reviewers toward adjacent mechanisms in other domains. A Physical AI assessment, for example, should not stop at physical safety when the same L4 neighbourhood also implicates General performance failure or Agentic authority.

Limitations

The network is constructed from text embeddings and a fixed nearest-neighbour rule. It is not an incident-propagation network, causal graph, or estimate of probability, severity, exposure, or regulatory priority. Wording changes, embedding models, thresholds, and the card inventory can change the fitted tail and rankings.

Methods and interpretive standard

  1. Clauset, A., Shalizi, C. R., & Newman, M. E. J. (2009). Power-law distributions in empirical data. SIAM Review, 51(4), 661–703. Basis for maximum-likelihood estimation, KS cutoff selection, bootstrap goodness-of-fit and likelihood-ratio comparison.
  2. Broido, A. D., & Clauset, A. (2019). Scale-free networks are rare. Nature Communications, 10, 1017. Basis for avoiding a scale-free claim when a power-law tail is limited or alternatives fit equally well.
  3. Alstott, J., Bullmore, E., & Plenz, D. (2014). powerlaw: A Python package for analysis of heavy-tailed distributions. PLOS ONE, 9(1), e85777. Practical reference for reproducible heavy-tail comparison.
  4. Newman, M. E. J. (2005). Power laws, Pareto distributions and Zipf's law. Contemporary Physics, 46(5), 323–351. Mathematical background on power-law distributions and their interpretation.
